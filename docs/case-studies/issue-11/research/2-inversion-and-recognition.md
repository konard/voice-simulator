# Running the voice simulator in reverse: non-neural inversion and recognition

_Scope and method (research date: November 2026). Survey of algorithms without neural networks for the reverse direction: audio -> speaker -> articulation -> IPA -> language -> text.
Every DOI was confirmed against the Crossref API; every other URL was opened or appeared as a search result during this research.
Accuracy numbers come only from pages, abstracts or PDFs that were read; snippet-level or secondhand sources are flagged, and unconfirmed items are marked **(unverified)** and collected in section 10.
Mechanism summaries combine the cited papers with textbook knowledge; costs marked "estimate" are our own operation counts, not measurements._

Conventions: `N` = samples per frame, `p` = LPC order, `M` = tube sections, `K` = frequency points, `T` = frames. "Per second" estimates assume 16 kHz audio and 100 frames per second unless stated.

### 1.2 Formant tracking

- **Praat (Burg).** Per frame: resample, pre-emphasise, Gaussian-like window, Burg LPC, roots to formants ([manual](https://www.fon.hum.uva.nl/praat/manual/Sound__To_Formant__burg____.html)). The window's effective length is twice the nominal length and the number of poles is twice the maximum number of formants. A separate tracking command adds a Viterbi pass over candidates (manual page not fetched, **unverified**).
- **ESPS / Snack / WaveSurfer.** Talkin 1987, "Speech formant trajectory estimation using dynamic programming with modulated transition costs", JASA 82(S1):S55, abstract only ([doi](https://doi.org/10.1121/1.2024869)). LPC pole candidates per frame, dynamic programming picks the smoothest plausible assignment.
- **Welling and Ney 1998**, IEEE TSAP 6(1):36-48 ([doi](https://doi.org/10.1109/89.650308)). No peak picking: the frequency axis is split into one segment per formant, each modelled by a digital resonator, and the segment boundaries are optimised by dynamic programming. Designed to give formants robust enough to use as recognition features.
- **KARMA.** Mehta, Rudoy, Wolfe 2012, JASA 132(3):1732-1746 ([doi](https://doi.org/10.1121/1.4739462), [arXiv PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1107.0076), earlier version at [Interspeech 2007](https://www.isca-archive.org/interspeech_2007/rudoy07_interspeech.html)). Observations are ARMA cepstral coefficients per frame; the hidden state holds formant and antiformant centre frequencies and bandwidths; an extended Kalman filter or smoother tracks them and returns uncertainty. Filtering-only operation is causal and therefore usable for streaming. The authors report that a particle filter performed worse in their simulations. No run times are given.
- **Reference data.** Deng et al. 2006, VTR formant database, ICASSP ([doi](https://doi.org/10.1109/icassp.2006.1660034)); Deng, Lee, Attias, Acero 2007, adaptive Kalman filtering for vocal-tract resonances, IEEE TASLP 15(1) ([doi](https://doi.org/10.1109/tasl.2006.876724)).

Accuracy seen (KARMA paper, Table 3, RMSE against the VTR database, 516 utterances):

| Tracker    | F1 (Hz) | F2 (Hz) | F3 (Hz) | Overall (Hz) |
| ---------- | ------- | ------- | ------- | ------------ |
| KARMA      | 114     | 226     | 320     | 220          |
| WaveSurfer | 170     | 276     | 383     | 276          |
| Praat      | 185     | 254     | 303     | 247          |

The practical message: even good non-neural trackers are wrong by 100-300 Hz RMS on real speech, so any inversion that consumes formants must treat them as noisy observations with a variance, not as exact values.

### 1.4 Voice activity and noise robustness

- **Energy plus zero-crossing rate.** Rabiner and Sambur 1975, Bell Syst. Tech. J. 54(2):297-315 ([doi](https://doi.org/10.1002/j.1538-7305.1975.tb02840.x)). Adaptive thresholds on short-time energy and zero crossings; `O(N)`.
- **G.729 Annex B.** Benyassine et al. 1997, IEEE Communications Magazine ([doi](https://doi.org/10.1109/35.620527)). Fixed decision rule on a handful of frame features with background-noise tracking and hang-over smoothing.
- **Statistical model-based VAD.** Sohn, Kim, Sung 1999, IEEE SPL 6(1):1-3 ([doi](https://doi.org/10.1109/97.736233)). Likelihood-ratio test per frequency bin under Gaussian speech and noise models, with an HMM-style hang-over.
- **Noise suppression.** Boll 1979, spectral subtraction, IEEE TASSP 27(2):113-120 ([doi](https://doi.org/10.1109/tassp.1979.1163209)); Ephraim and Malah 1984, MMSE short-time spectral amplitude, IEEE TASSP 32(6) ([doi](https://doi.org/10.1109/tassp.1984.1164453)). Both need a running noise spectrum estimated in non-speech frames; cost is one FFT pair per frame.
- **Channel normalisation.** Atal 1974, JASA 55(6) ([doi](https://doi.org/10.1121/1.1914702)) introduced cepstral mean removal; Viikki and Laurila 1998, Speech Communication 25(1-3):133-147 ([doi](<https://doi.org/10.1016/s0167-6393(98)00033-8>)) describe segmental mean and variance normalisation suited to on-line use.

### 1.5 How the front end would be used here

- Run one shared analysis at a 5 ms hop: pre-emphasis, Burg LPC (order about `fs/1000 + 2`), FFT, mel energies, f0 and HNR by the Boersma method, energy-plus-ZCR voice activity. Estimated total below 2e7 operations per second of audio, far inside a real-time budget.
- Keep reflection coefficients, not only formants, because they feed the tube-shaped initial guess in section 3.2.
- Upgrade path: pYIN-style Viterbi for f0, Kalman (KARMA-style) formant tracking with variances, Sohn-type VAD, sliding-window cepstral mean normalisation.

### 2.2 Glottal closure instants

- DYPSA: Naylor et al. 2007, IEEE TASLP 15(1) ([doi](https://doi.org/10.1109/tasl.2006.876878)). Phase-slope function of the LPC residual, then dynamic programming.
- SEDREAMS: Drugman and Dutoit 2009, Interspeech pp. 2891-2894 ([doi](https://doi.org/10.21437/interspeech.2009-47)). A mean-based signal gives a short search interval per period; the residual peak inside it is the instant.
- Zero-frequency filtering: Murty and Yegnanarayana 2008, IEEE TASLP 16(8):1602-1613 ([doi](https://doi.org/10.1109/tasl.2008.2004526)). Pass speech through cascaded resonators at 0 Hz, remove the trend; positive zero crossings are epochs.
- Early covariance-determinant approach: Strube 1974, JASA 56(5):1625-1629 ([doi](https://doi.org/10.1121/1.1903487)).
- Quantitative review: Drugman, Thomas, Gudnason, Naylor, Dutoit 2012, IEEE TASLP 20(3):994-1006 ([doi](https://doi.org/10.1109/tasl.2011.2170835), [arXiv 2001.00473](https://arxiv.org/abs/2001.00473)).

### 2.3 Source model fitting and voice-quality measures

- **LF model and Rd.** Fant's single shape parameter Rd summarises the Liljencrants-Fant pulse; the retrievable reference is Fant 1997, "The LF-model revisited" ([doi](https://doi.org/10.1515/9783110809411.77)); the 1995 STL-QPSR report has no Crossref DOI (**unverified**). Südholt et al. 2023 (section 3.6) derive Rd from H1-H2 through Fant's linear relation and set tenseness `T = 1 - Rd/3`.
- **Fitting methods.** Degottex, Roebel, Rodet 2011, phase minimisation, IEEE TASLP 19(5):1080-1090 ([doi](https://doi.org/10.1109/tasl.2010.2076806)); Kane and Gobl 2013, Speech Communication 55(3):397-414 ([doi](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.specom.2012.12.004)); Kane, Yanushevskaya, Ní Chasaide, Gobl 2012, Speech Prosody ([doi](https://doi.org/10.21437/speechprosody.2012-39)). Toolbox: COVAREP, Degottex et al. 2014, ICASSP ([doi](https://doi.org/10.1109/icassp.2014.6853739), [repository](https://github.com/covarep/covarep)).
- **Spectral measures.** H1-H2 and H1-A3: Hanson 1997, JASA 101(1):466-481 ([doi](https://doi.org/10.1121/1.417991)); formant-corrected versions: Iseli, Shue, Alwan 2007, JASA 121(4):2283-2295 ([doi](https://doi.org/10.1121/1.2697522)). Cepstral peak prominence: Hillenbrand, Cleveland, Erickson 1994, JSHR 37(4):769-778 ([doi](https://doi.org/10.1044/jshr.3704.769)). HNR: Boersma 1993 (section 1.3).
- **Time-domain measures.** Normalised amplitude quotient: Alku, Bäckström, Vilkman 2002, JASA 112(2):701-710 ([doi](https://doi.org/10.1121/1.1490365)). Parabolic spectral parameter: Alku, Strik, Vilkman 1997, Speech Communication 22(1):67-79 ([doi](<https://doi.org/10.1016/s0167-6393(97)00020-4>)). Jitter and shimmer follow from the closure-instant sequence.

### 2.4 How it would be used here

- First version: keep the existing H1-H2, HNR and f0 statistics, add cepstral peak prominence and formant-corrected H1-H2, and map them to the simulator's source parameters.
- Upgrade: SEDREAMS or zero-frequency epochs, then IAIF (no epochs needed) or QCP (with epochs) to obtain a glottal flow derivative; fit the simulator's own source model to it by least squares on a few pitch periods. Averaging over many periods in pass 1 makes the fit stable.
- The inverse-filtered tract response is also the cleanest input for tract inversion: the Südholt et al. experiment shows that inverse-filter error, not the optimiser, dominates the final error (section 3.6).

## 3. Acoustic-to-articulatory inversion without neural networks

### 3.1 Why the problem is ill-posed

- Atal, Chang, Mathews, Tukey 1978, JASA 63(5):1535-1555 ([doi](https://doi.org/10.1121/1.381848)) sorted the outputs of an articulatory model by acoustic result and found several distinct tract configurations with the same first three formants for each vowel tested.
- Mermelstein 1967, JASA 41(5):1283-1294 ([doi](https://doi.org/10.1121/1.1910470)) and Schroeder 1967, JASA 41(4B):1002-1010 ([doi](https://doi.org/10.1121/1.1910429)): formant frequencies alone fix only part of the area function; a unique solution needs a second set of data such as the lip impedance, which speech does not provide.
- Sondhi and Gopinath 1971, JASA 49(6B):1867-1873 ([doi](https://doi.org/10.1121/1.1912593)) and Sondhi and Resnick 1983, JASA 73(3):985-1002 ([doi](https://doi.org/10.1121/1.389024)): the area function is recoverable from an acoustic impulse response measured at the lips. This is a calibration method, not a speech method.
- Consequence: inversion from speech must add information: articulatory model constraints, continuity over time, speaker anatomy fixed in pass 1, and phonetic or lexical priors.

### 3.2 Direct estimates: LPC lattice equals Kelly-Lochbaum tube

- Wakita 1973, IEEE Trans. Audio and Electroacoustics 21(5):417-427 ([doi](https://doi.org/10.1109/tau.1973.1162506)). The abstract states that the inverse (LPC) filter and a discrete acoustic tube are mathematically identical with the tube's reflection coefficients as the common quantity, so reflection coefficients obtained by inverse filtering give a discrete area function directly.
- The Kelly-Lochbaum model (1962) is the same structure seen from the synthesis side: a ladder of scattering junctions, later called a digital waveguide filter ([J. O. Smith, Physical Audio Signal Processing](https://ccrma.stanford.edu/~jos/pasp/Singing_Kelly_Lochbaum_Vocal_Tract.html)). Kelly and Lochbaum 1962 has no Crossref DOI.
- Recipe: remove source and radiation tilt, run Burg LPC with `p = M` at the sampling rate where one section equals one sample of two-way travel (`fs = c M / (2 L)`, a textbook relation: about 16 kHz for 16 sections of a 17 cm tract), then convert each reflection coefficient to an area ratio `(1 - k) / (1 + k)` (sign convention differs between texts; see Markel and Gray 1976).
- Known limits: Wakita's derivation assumes a glottal end terminated in its characteristic impedance and zero impedance at the lips; Kalgaonkar and Clements study how non-ideal boundary conditions distort the estimate ([Interspeech 2007 PDF](https://www.isca-archive.org/interspeech_2007/kalgaonkar07_interspeech.pdf)). The estimate also depends on correct removal of the source spectrum, on losses, on tract length and on the absence of nasal or side branches.
- Cost: nothing beyond the LPC already computed. This is why it is the recommended initial guess even though it is not trustworthy on its own.

### 3.3 Codebooks and dynamic programming

- Schroeter and Sondhi 1994, IEEE TSAP 2(1):133-150 ([doi](https://doi.org/10.1109/89.260356)): review of acoustic-to-geometry mapping. They identify the initialisation of the optimisation loop as the major stumbling block and recommend starting from a pre-computed articulatory codebook (as quoted in a search result; full text not re-read).
- Ouni and Laprie 2005, hypercube codebook, JASA 118(1):444-460 ([doi](https://doi.org/10.1121/1.1921448)): the articulatory space of Maeda's model is cut into hypercubes inside which the articulatory-to-acoustic map is nearly linear; finer cubes where it is not. An [earlier Eurospeech 2001 paper](https://www.isca-archive.org/eurospeech_2001/ouni01_eurospeech.pdf) describes the idea.
- Potard, Laprie, Ouni 2008, JASA 123(4):2310-2323 ([doi](https://doi.org/10.1121/1.2885747); conference version [arXiv cs/0511076](https://arxiv.org/pdf/cs/0511076)): adds phonetic constraints to prune implausible codebook solutions.
- Laprie and Mathieu 1998, ICASSP ([doi](https://doi.org/10.1109/icassp.1998.675418)): variational formulation; the trajectory minimises acoustic error plus a smoothness functional.
- Maeda's articulatory model: Maeda 1990, book chapter ([doi](https://doi.org/10.1007/978-94-009-2037-8_6)).
- Mechanism: for each frame retrieve all codebook entries whose formants (or spectrum) lie within a tolerance; a Viterbi pass selects one entry per frame minimising acoustic distance plus articulatory jump cost. Cost: lookup `O(log C)` per frame with a spatial index over `C` entries, Viterbi `O(T B^2)` for `B` candidates per frame.
- Other model-based systems for context: Shirai and Kobayashi 1986 ([doi](<https://doi.org/10.1016/0167-6393(86)90005-1>)); Sorokin and Trushkin 1996 ([doi](<https://doi.org/10.1016/0167-6393(96)00028-3>)); Sorokin, Leonov, Trushkin 2000 ([doi](<https://doi.org/10.1016/s0167-6393(99)00031-x>)); Yehia and Itakura 1996 ([doi](<https://doi.org/10.1016/0167-6393(95)00042-9>)); Dang and Honda 2002 ([doi](https://doi.org/10.1006/jpho.2002.0167)); Schoentgen and Ciocea 1997 ([doi](<https://doi.org/10.1016/s0167-6393(97)00007-1>)).

### 3.4 Analysis-by-synthesis optimisation

- **Formant-space optimisation with regularisation.** Panchapagesan and Alwan 2011, JASA 129(4):2144-2162 ([doi](https://doi.org/10.1121/1.3514544); conference version [doi](https://doi.org/10.21437/interspeech.2008-697)). Vowels only; Maeda model calibrated to two speakers of the X-ray microbeam database; cost is distance on the first three formants plus regularisation and continuity terms; the optimiser is started from a fast codebook search (from search-result summaries; error figures not seen).
- **Sensitivity functions.** Story 2006, JASA 119(2):715-718 ([doi](https://doi.org/10.1121/1.2151802)): for the current area function compute, per formant, the difference between kinetic and potential energy along the tract (after Fant and Pauli); scale each by the gap between desired and current formant, sum, and perturb the area function; iterate. The author presents it as a tuning tool for an existing shape, not as full inversion. Related: Mrayati, Carré, Guérin 1988, distinctive regions and modes ([doi](<https://doi.org/10.1016/0167-6393(88)90073-8>)).
- **Derivative-free optimisers.** Nelder and Mead 1965 ([doi](https://doi.org/10.1093/comjnl/7.4.308)); Kirkpatrick et al. 1983, simulated annealing ([doi](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.220.4598.671)); Hansen and Ostermeier 2001, CMA-ES ([doi](https://doi.org/10.1162/106365601750190398)). Our searches found no articulatory-inversion paper built on CMA-ES or Bayesian optimisation, so their suitability here is our inference, not a literature result.
- **Genetic algorithms.** McGowan 1994, task dynamics plus a genetic algorithm, Speech Communication 14(1):19-48 ([doi](<https://doi.org/10.1016/0167-6393(94)90055-8>)). Gao, Stone, Birkholz 2019, Interspeech pp. 3770-3774 ([doi](https://doi.org/10.21437/interspeech.2019-1334), [abstract](https://www.isca-archive.org/interspeech_2019/gao19e_interspeech.html)): a rule-based initial gestural score for [VocalTractLab](https://www.vocaltractlab.de/) is refined by a genetic algorithm that minimises a cosine distance between acoustic features, with parameter ranges limited; the abstract reports that it beat a coordinate-descent baseline.
- **Other copy-synthesis work.** Prom-on, Birkholz, Xu 2014, EURASIP JASMP ([doi](https://doi.org/10.1186/1687-4722-2014-23)): vowel targets learned by stochastic optimisation against MFCC error. Nam et al. 2012, JASA 132(6):3980-3989 ([doi](https://doi.org/10.1121/1.4763545)): gestural scores for the TADA task-dynamics synthesiser are time-warped iteratively until the synthetic acoustics match; reported as better than plain acoustic dynamic time warping. Cámara, Blanco, Reiss 2025, EURASIP JASMP ([doi](https://doi.org/10.1186/s13636-025-00414-5)): parameter optimisation for a simplified waveguide voice model (abstract only). Krug, Stone, Birkholz 2021 ([doi](https://doi.org/10.21437/ssw.2021-18)) measure intelligibility and naturalness of VocalTractLab synthesis, and Van Niekerk et al. 2022 ([doi](https://doi.org/10.21437/interspeech.2022-10689)) study exploration strategies for complex syllable onsets (titles only). VocalTractLab source: [TUD-STKS/VocalTractLab-dev](https://github.com/TUD-STKS/VocalTractLab-dev) (GPL-3.0, so ideas may be reused but code may not be copied into a public-domain project).
- Cost (estimate): one frequency-domain evaluation of an `M`-section tube at `K` frequencies is `M * K` two-by-two complex products, about 1e5 operations for `M = 44`, `K = 64`. One second of time-domain one-dimensional waveguide synthesis at 44.1 kHz is about 1e7 operations. A population method with hundreds of candidates per frame is therefore affordable only in the frequency domain; full three-dimensional simulation cannot sit inside the search loop.

### 3.5 Dynamic and filtering formulations

- Kalman-type trackers treat articulatory or resonance parameters as a smooth hidden state and the acoustic features as nonlinear observations (KARMA and Deng et al. 2007 in section 1.2 are worked examples in resonance space). Cost: one Jacobian and a small matrix update per frame.
- Dusan and Deng 2000 (extended Kalman filtering with phonological constraints, ICSLP) is the usual articulatory-space reference; we could not find a DOI or an archive page (**unverified**).
- Generalised smoothness: Ghosh and Narayanan 2010, JASA 128(4):2162-2172 ([doi](https://doi.org/10.1121/1.3455847)). Each articulator gets its own data-driven smoothness filter; the abstract reports an improvement on MOCHA-TIMIT over a fixed constraint and a recursive (streaming-capable) form without loss of accuracy (numbers not seen).
- Particle filters handle multi-modal posteriors, which is what non-uniqueness produces, at `O(P)` forward evaluations per frame for `P` particles. Evidence is mixed: the KARMA authors report worse results than the extended Kalman filter in their setting.

### 3.6 Gradient descent through a differentiable waveguide

- Südholt, Cámara, Xu, Reiss 2023, "Vocal tract area estimation by gradient descent", DAFx 2023 ([arXiv 2307.04702](https://arxiv.org/abs/2307.04702); code: [vocal-tract-grad](https://github.com/davidmarttila/vocal-tract-grad)). No Crossref DOI.
- Method: inverse filter the frame (GFM-IAIF) to get the tract response and glottal flow derivative; f0 by YIN; source tenseness from H1-H2; then gradient descent on tongue position, tongue diameter and constrictions, which map to a 44-section area function. The loss is the mean squared difference of log magnitudes between the closed-form Kelly-Lochbaum transfer function and the inverse-filtered response. 100 steps per frame, warm-started from the previous frame. The proposed method itself contains no neural network.
- Results read from the paper: with the true tract response as target and no constriction, mean absolute error is 0.13 dB in frequency response and 0.02 cm in tongue diameter; with the inverse-filtered target it rises to 2.09 dB and 0.26 cm. With one or two constrictions the frequency-response error is 0.60 or 0.87 dB (true target) and 2.33 or 2.50 dB (inverse-filtered). In a listening test with 22 participants the method was rated significantly above a genetic algorithm, particle swarm optimisation and a neural network trained on one million parameter sets. No timings are reported.
- Stated limits: open vowels only, fixed male-length tract, non-uniqueness, and the inverse-filtering error.
- For this project the gradient can be hand-written: the transfer function is a product of two-by-two matrices, so a reverse sweep (adjoint) gives all `M` area derivatives for about three times the cost of one evaluation. No automatic-differentiation library and no network is needed.

### 3.7 Statistical mappings that need articulatory data (for contrast)

- Toda, Black, Tokuda 2008, GMM mapping with dynamic features, Speech Communication 50(3):215-227 ([doi](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.specom.2007.09.001)); the [Interspeech 2004 version](https://www.isca-archive.org/interspeech_2004/toda04_interspeech.html) reports, per a search-result summary, RMS errors of 1.61 mm (female) and 1.53 mm (male) with 32 mixtures.
- Hiroya and Honda 2004, HMM-based production model, IEEE TSAP 12(2):175-185 ([doi](https://doi.org/10.1109/tsa.2003.822636)).
- Richmond, King, Taylor 2003 ([doi](<https://doi.org/10.1016/s0885-2308(03)00005-6>)) is the neural counterpart, listed only to mark the boundary.
- These are not neural, but they require measured articulator positions for training. In this project the simulator can generate the paired data instead, which turns them into a compact form of codebook.

### 3.8 How inversion would be used here

- Pass 1 fixes what is constant: tract length, nasal and head geometry, source type. That removes the largest source of ambiguity before frame-wise inversion starts.
- Per frame in sonorant regions: tube shape from reflection coefficients as a sanity prior, codebook lookup in the simulator's own control space (tongue, jaw, lips, velum), Viterbi continuity, then a few adjoint-gradient or Nelder-Mead steps on the log-spectral loss.
- Inversion output should be a short list of weighted articulatory hypotheses per frame, never a single answer; the phone and word stages resolve the rest.
- Obstruents are handled by landmark cues (section 4.3) and verified by synthesis, not by tube inversion, because an all-pole tube cannot represent frication sources, bursts or nasal zeros.

### 4.2 Speaker adaptation

- Vocal-tract length normalisation: Lee and Rose 1998, IEEE TSAP 6(1):49-60 ([doi](https://doi.org/10.1109/89.650310)) choose a frequency-warp factor by maximum likelihood over a small grid; Eide and Gish 1996, ICASSP ([doi](https://doi.org/10.1109/icassp.1996.541103)) derive it from formant positions.
- MLLR: Leggetter and Woodland 1995 ([doi](https://doi.org/10.1006/csla.1995.0010)), affine transforms of Gaussian means. Constrained MLLR (feature-space): Gales 1998 ([doi](https://doi.org/10.1006/csla.1998.0043)). MAP adaptation: Gauvain and Lee 1994 ([doi](https://doi.org/10.1109/89.279278)).
- In this project pass 1 already estimates tract length physically; that is a parametric form of VTLN and should be used to generate speaker-specific templates instead of warping features.

### 4.3 Landmarks, distinctive features, articulatory features and gestures

- Stevens 2002, JASA 111(4):1872-1891 ([doi](https://doi.org/10.1121/1.1458026)): lexical access from features. Find landmarks (abrupt consonantal events, glides, vowel peaks), measure acoustic cues near each, estimate distinctive-feature bundles, match bundles to a lexicon stored as features, and verify by internal synthesis.
- Liu 1996, JASA 100(5):3417-3430 ([doi](https://doi.org/10.1121/1.416983), [thesis record](https://dspace.mit.edu/handle/1721.1/11406), [Eurospeech 1995 abstract](https://www.isca-archive.org/eurospeech_1995/liu95_eurospeech.html)): abrupt energy changes in several frequency bands at two time resolutions give glottal, sonorant and burst landmarks. Cost: a few band energies and differences per millisecond.
- Juneja and Espy-Wilson 2008, JASA 123(2):1154-1168 ([doi](https://doi.org/10.1121/1.2823754), [thesis abstract](https://drum.lib.umd.edu/items/a4be15d3-4882-4855-9eee-6b9a37c49e0f/full)): binary support vector machines on knowledge-based acoustic parameters classify manner features (sonorant, syllabic, continuant); a probabilistic framework turns them into landmark sequences. Support vector machines, not neural networks.
- Hasegawa-Johnson et al. 2005, ICASSP pp. I-213-216 ([doi](https://doi.org/10.1109/icassp.2005.1415088), [PDF](https://sls.csail.mit.edu/archives/root/publications/2005/hasegawa-johnson_etal_ICASSP05.pdf), [workshop page](https://www.clsp.jhu.edu/workshops/04-workshop/landmark-based-speech-recognition/)): support vector machines for landmarks and features plus three pronunciation models (dynamic programming, a dynamic Bayesian network of articulatory phonology, maximum entropy), used to rescore lattices. No neural networks.
- Feature-based pronunciation models: Livescu and Glass 2004, HLT-NAACL ([doi](https://doi.org/10.3115/1613984.1614005)) and Interspeech 2004 ([doi](https://doi.org/10.21437/interspeech.2004-269)): articulatory feature streams may desynchronise within limits, modelled by a dynamic Bayesian network. Livescu et al. 2007, ICASSP ([doi](https://doi.org/10.1109/icassp.2007.366989)) summarises the 2006 workshop (some of its feature classifiers were multilayer perceptrons, **unverified**). Reviews: King et al. 2007, JASA ([doi](https://doi.org/10.1121/1.2404622)); Frankel, Wester, King 2007 ([doi](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.csl.2007.03.002)).
- Gesture-based recognition: Mitra et al. 2010, IEEE JSTSP 4(6) ([doi](https://doi.org/10.1109/jstsp.2010.2076013)) and 2011, IEEE TASLP 19(7):1913-1924 ([doi](https://doi.org/10.1109/tasl.2010.2103058)). The tract-variable estimator in the 2011 paper is a neural network (confirmed from the abstract); adding its outputs to acoustic features improved noisy-digit recognition. Later work treats gestures as hidden variables of a dynamic Bayesian network. The non-neural part of that line is Nam et al. 2012 (section 3.4).

### 4.4 Syllable nuclei and segmentation

- Mermelstein 1975, JASA 58(4):880-883 ([doi](https://doi.org/10.1121/1.380738)): a loudness minimum is a syllable boundary if the gap between the loudness curve and its convex hull is large enough; secondary sources describe the input as energy between 500 Hz and 4 kHz. Cost `O(T)`.
- de Jong and Wempe 2009, Behavior Research Methods 41(2):385-390 ([doi](https://doi.org/10.3758/brm.41.2.385)): intensity peaks preceded and followed by dips are candidate nuclei; unvoiced peaks are discarded. The abstract reports high correlations with human syllable counts on two Dutch corpora (values not seen). A related Praat script repository is [hugoquene/tempo](https://github.com/hugoquene/tempo) (GPL-3.0).
- Wang and Narayanan 2007, IEEE TASLP 15(8):2190-2201 ([doi](https://doi.org/10.1109/tasl.2007.905178)): temporal and sub-band spectral correlation sharpen the syllable envelope; no recogniser needed; evaluated on Switchboard.
- Phone boundaries: Dusan and Rabiner 2006, Interspeech ([doi](https://doi.org/10.21437/interspeech.2006-230)) relate maxima of spectral transition to boundaries; Räsänen, Laine, Altosaar 2011 ([doi](https://doi.org/10.5772/16433)) review blind segmentation and define the R-value quality measure ([doi](https://doi.org/10.21437/interspeech.2009-538), [PDF](https://www.isca-archive.org/interspeech_2009/rasanen09b_interspeech.pdf)).
- Bayesian information criterion: Chen and Gopalakrishnan 1998, ICASSP ([doi](https://doi.org/10.1109/icassp.1998.675347)): a window is split where two Gaussians explain it better than one after a complexity penalty. Used for speaker and environment changes.

### 4.5 Analysis by synthesis and motor theory

- Halle and Stevens 1962, IRE Trans. Information Theory 8(2):155-159 ([doi](https://doi.org/10.1109/tit.1962.1057686)): recognise by generating candidate signals with internal rules and comparing them with the input; a preliminary analysis limits the candidates.
- Bever and Poeppel 2010, Biolinguistics 4(2-3):174-200 ([doi](https://doi.org/10.5964/bioling.8783)) and Poeppel and Monahan 2011 ([doi](https://doi.org/10.1080/01690965.2010.493301)) revisit the idea as a hypothesise-and-test loop with Bayesian weighting.
- Liberman and Mattingly 1985, Cognition 21(1):1-36 ([doi](<https://doi.org/10.1016/0010-0277(85)90021-6>)): the motor theory, in which the objects of perception are intended articulatory gestures. This is the conceptual licence for recognising through a production model.

### 4.7 How it would be used here

- Use the landmark and syllable-nucleus layer as the segmentation backbone; it is cheap, causal within about 100 ms, and gives the live "syllable detected" event.
- Use the simulator as the acoustic model: generate templates or Gaussian statistics per phone and context for the fitted speaker, then score with dynamic time warping or a small GMM-HMM. This is non-neural, needs no recorded corpus, and inherits speaker adaptation for free. The risk is the gap between simulated and real speech (section 9).
- Keep everything as weighted lattices so that later stages (language, lexicon) can overrule the acoustic guess.

## 5. From phones to language and text

### 5.1 Language identification from phone sequences

- Zissman 1996, IEEE TSAP 4(1):31-44 ([doi](https://doi.org/10.1109/tsa.1996.481450), [abstract](https://www.ll.mit.edu/r-d/publications/comparison-four-approaches-automatic-language-identification-telephone-speech)). Phone recognition followed by language modelling: decode phones once, score the sequence with one phone n-gram model per language, pick the best. The parallel variant runs several phone recognisers and fuses scores.
- Numbers from the abstract (telephone speech corpus): the parallel system was best, with 2 % error on 45 s and 5 % on 10 s utterances for two-language closed-set tests, and 11 % and 21 % for eleven languages.
- Cost: n-gram lookups per phone; negligible.
- For this project: one universal IPA decoder plus four phone trigram models built by running the existing text-to-IPA front end over text corpora. Add direct cues: presence of lexical tone contours, retroflex and aspirated stops, palatalised consonants. Expect short utterances to be unreliable, as the 10 s figures show.

### 5.2 Mixed-language speech

- Lyu and Lyu 2008, Interspeech pp. 711-714 ([doi](https://doi.org/10.21437/interspeech.2008-223)): Mandarin-Taiwanese code-switching identified with acoustic, prosodic and phonetic cues combined by a maximum a posteriori rule over acoustic, duration and language models (accuracy not seen).
- Practical design: score languages per syllable or word window with a hidden Markov chain over language labels (a switching penalty prevents flicker), and decode words with the union of lexicons, each word tagged with its language.

### 5.3 Phone lattice to words

- Compose the phone lattice with a confusion transducer (substitution, insertion and deletion costs derived from articulatory distance between phones), the pronunciation lexicon and a word n-gram model, as in Mohri et al. 2002 (section 4.1). A lexicon trie with beam search is the simple equivalent.
- Lexicon source: run the project's own text-to-IPA rules over a frequency-ranked word list per language; store all variants. This guarantees that the recogniser's phone inventory matches the synthesiser's.
- Phoneme-to-grapheme for out-of-lexicon words: Bisani and Ney 2008, joint-sequence models, Speech Communication 50(5):434-451 ([doi](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.specom.2008.01.002)): pairs of letter and phone chunks ("graphones") modelled by a smoothed n-gram, usable in both directions. Implementations: [Sequitur G2P](https://github.com/sequitur-g2p/sequitur-g2p) (GPL-2.0, described by its repository as abandonware) and [Phonetisaurus](https://github.com/AdolfVonKleist/Phonetisaurus) (BSD-3-Clause), Novak, Minematsu, Hirose, Natural Language Engineering ([doi](https://doi.org/10.1017/s1351324915000315)), which builds the same model as transducers. Error rates were not seen.
- Homophones are resolved only by the word n-gram model (section 4.1); without it the output should show the most frequent spelling.

### 5.4 Mandarin tone

- Xu 1997, Journal of Phonetics 25(1):61-83 ([doi](https://doi.org/10.1006/jpho.1996.0034)) and Xu and Wang 2001 ([doi](<https://doi.org/10.1016/s0167-6393(00)00063-7>)): tone contours are approached asymptotically within each syllable and are heavily shaped by the preceding tone, so the informative part is the later portion of the syllable.
- Prom-on, Xu, Thipakorn 2009, JASA 125(1):405-424 ([doi](https://doi.org/10.1121/1.3037222), [model page](https://www.homepages.ucl.ac.uk/~uclyyix/qTA/)): the quantitative target approximation model. Each syllable has a linear pitch target (height and slope) and a strength; f0 is the response of a critically damped system (order **unverified**) approaching that target, with state carried across syllable boundaries. Parameters were learned by analysis by synthesis. Inversion fits three numbers per syllable by least squares, then classifies the target (high, rising, low, falling).
- Statistical recognisers: Yang, Lee, Chang, Wang 1988, HMM tone recognition, IEEE TASSP 36(7):988-992 ([doi](https://doi.org/10.1109/29.1620)); Zhang and Hirose 2004, tone nucleus model, Speech Communication 42(3-4):447-466 ([doi](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.specom.2004.01.001)). Reported figures seen in abstracts: 86.34 % for continuous Mandarin with a semi-continuous HMM on orthonormal-transform coefficients of the syllable's f0 and energy contours (Wang, Shieh, Chen 1994, International Journal of Pattern Recognition and Artificial Intelligence 8(1):233-246, [doi](https://doi.org/10.1142/S0218001494000115); abstract via search result); later tone-nucleus work that reached 87.1 % speaker-dependent used a neural network and is outside scope.
- Cost: a least-squares fit per syllable; negligible. Tone plus syllable gives pinyin; characters need the word n-gram model.

### 5.5 Russian and Hindi specifics

- Russian: grapheme-to-phoneme mapping is not one-to-one, and stress position floats between word forms and is phonemic ([Kipyatkova, Karpov, Verkhodanova, Železný 2012, FedCSIS](https://annals-csis.org/proceedings/2012/pliks/148.pdf), from a search-result summary); sounds also reduce or assimilate in spontaneous speech, which that group handles with multiple transcriptions per word. The acoustic stage will hear reduced vowels; the spelling can only be recovered through a stress-marked lexicon and the language model. Stress itself is detectable as longer duration and less reduced vowel quality, which should be used as a lexicon-matching feature and not as a hard decision.
- Hindi: the inherent schwa is written but often not pronounced. Narasimhan, Sproat, Kiraz 2004, International Journal of Speech Technology 7(4):319-333 ([doi](https://doi.org/10.1023/b:ijst.0000037075.71599.62)) reach 89 % with rules, as reported by a later paper ([arXiv 2004.10353](https://arxiv.org/abs/2004.10353)), which also quotes 99.80 % word-level accuracy on native words for the diachronic rules of Choudhury, Basu, Sarkar 2004 ([doi](https://doi.org/10.3115/1622153.1622156)); see also Tyson and Nagar 2009 ([doi](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10772-009-9040-x)). For recognition, list both the schwa-deleted and the schwa-retained pronunciation of ambiguous words so that rule errors do not become recognition errors.

## 6. Streaming and multi-pass design

- **Multi-pass search.** Schwartz and Austin 1991, ICASSP ([doi](https://doi.org/10.1109/icassp.1991.150436)): keep the N best hypotheses from a cheap pass and rescore them with expensive knowledge. Ney and Ortmanns 2000 (section 4.1): word graphs as the interface between passes. Here the expensive knowledge is the physical synthesiser.
- **Speaker-adaptive training.** Anastasakos et al. 1996, ICSLP ([doi](https://doi.org/10.21437/icslp.1996-253)): alternate between estimating per-speaker transforms and speaker-independent models. The project analogue is alternating between head parameters and articulation.
- **On-line adaptation.** Digalakis 1999, IEEE TSAP 7(3):253-261 ([doi](https://doi.org/10.1109/89.759031)); Huo and Lee 1997 ([doi](https://doi.org/10.1109/89.554778)): recursive Bayesian updates of model parameters as unlabelled data arrives. The general principle is expectation-maximisation, Dempster, Laird, Rubin 1977 ([doi](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.2517-6161.1977.tb01600.x)).
- **A working non-neural example.** Kaldi's GMM online decoder ([documentation](https://kaldi-asr.org/doc/online_decoding.html), read): cepstral mean normalisation over a sliding window of 6 s by default; basis-fMLLR speaker transform first estimated 2 s into the first utterance and re-estimated at intervals growing by a factor of 1.5 (later utterances at 5, 10, 20 s) and at utterance end; partial results reported every 1 s by default and liable to change until the utterance ends.
- **Caption latency.** Regulator targets as summarised in search results: 3 s (United Kingdom) and 5-6 s (Canada) (**unverified** at source). A 2026 study with 216 deaf and hard-of-hearing participants found that the usual 7-12 s delay of broadcast captions significantly harms the experience and modelled recogniser captions with a 2 s average delay ([arXiv 2609.11408](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.11408), abstract read).

Recommended timing design (our proposal):

| Layer                             | Trigger                                                  | Target delay | Stability                                              |
| --------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------- | ------------ | ------------------------------------------------------ |
| Syllable highlight                | Nucleus detector confirms a peak                         | 100-200 ms   | Never retracted                                        |
| Provisional IPA for the syllable  | Fixed-lag Viterbi over inversion candidates              | 300-500 ms   | May change until the word closes                       |
| Provisional word and language tag | Lexicon beam                                             | 0.5-1.5 s    | May change until a pause                               |
| Final text                        | Pause or 5 s cap; rescoring by synthesis                 | under 2-3 s  | Final                                                  |
| Speaker parameters                | First 2-3 s of voiced speech, then slow recursive update | Background   | Frozen within an utterance, updated between utterances |

Stability rule taken from the Kaldi design: never change speaker parameters in the middle of an utterance; apply the new estimate at the next pause and, if it moved by more than a threshold, re-decode the buffered utterance once.

## 8. Recommended pipeline

The structural advantage of this project is that the synthesiser is a lossless-tube lattice, so LPC analysis is its approximate algebraic inverse and the synthesiser itself can serve as acoustic model, codebook generator and verifier. The pipeline below uses that in four places (stages 5, 6, 7 and 11).

| #   | Stage                    | Input -> output                                                             | First implementation                                                                                         | Later upgrade                                                                                      | Cost per second of audio (estimate)                         |
| --- | ------------------------ | --------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------- |
| 1   | Front end                | Samples -> frames with LPC, reflection coefficients, spectrum, mel energies | Pre-emphasis, 25 ms window, 5 ms hop, Burg LPC, FFT                                                          | Sliding cepstral mean normalisation, spectral subtraction, reassignment for bursts                 | About 1e7 operations                                        |
| 2   | Activity, pitch, voicing | Frames -> speech flag, f0, HNR, voiced flag                                 | Energy plus ZCR; Boersma autocorrelation                                                                     | Sohn-type VAD; pYIN-style Viterbi; SEDREAMS or zero-frequency epochs                               | About 1e7                                                   |
| 3   | Pass 1: speaker fit      | First seconds of voiced frames -> head and source parameters with variances | Existing statistics (f0, formants, tract length, H1-H2, HNR, rate) with derivative-free fit                  | IAIF or QCP source fit; maximum-likelihood tract length; recursive update between utterances       | Background task, seconds of CPU per update                  |
| 4   | Segmentation             | Frames -> landmarks, syllable nuclei, broad manner classes                  | Liu-style band-energy landmarks; Mermelstein or de Jong-Wempe nuclei; threshold rules                        | Probabilistic manner features; spectral-transition boundaries                                      | About 1e6                                                   |
| 5   | Pass 2: inversion        | Sonorant frames plus speaker parameters -> weighted articulatory candidates | Area from reflection coefficients as prior; codebook generated by the simulator; Viterbi continuity          | Adjoint gradient refinement of the tube transfer function; Kalman smoothing; multiple hypotheses   | 1e7 with codebook only; about 1e8-1e9 with refinement       |
| 6   | Consonant cues           | Landmark neighbourhoods -> place, manner, voicing scores                    | Burst spectrum, frication centroid, formant transitions, voice onset time, nasal murmur rules                | Templates synthesised for the fitted speaker and compared by dynamic time warping                  | About 1e6 plus template cost                                |
| 7   | Phone lattice            | Candidates and cues -> IPA lattice with confidences, aligned to syllables   | Per-syllable best match against simulator phone targets                                                      | Small GMM-HMM trained on simulator output; token passing                                           | About 1e7                                                   |
| 8   | Prosody                  | f0 and durations per syllable -> tone, stress, length                       | Normalised f0 height and slope rules                                                                         | Target-approximation fit per syllable                                                              | Negligible                                                  |
| 9   | Language                 | Lattice -> language posterior per window                                    | Phone trigram models per language plus cue rules                                                             | Hidden Markov chain over language for mixed speech                                                 | Negligible                                                  |
| 10  | Words                    | Lattice plus language -> words with times                                   | Lexicon trie from the project's own text-to-IPA rules, confusion-weighted edit distance, unigram frequencies | Kneser-Ney n-grams, transducer composition, joint-sequence spelling for unknown words              | 1e6-1e8 depending on beam                                   |
| 11  | Verification             | N-best words -> rescored result                                             | None                                                                                                         | Re-synthesise the top hypotheses with the fitted voice and compare spectra (analysis by synthesis) | One-dimensional model only: about 1e7 per hypothesis-second |
| 12  | Streaming control        | All stages -> stable live display                                           | Fixed-lag commit, speaker parameters frozen per utterance                                                    | Re-decode buffer after a large speaker update                                                      | Negligible                                                  |
| 13  | Voice copy check         | Fitted voice plus recognised articulation -> resynthesis and scores         | Log-spectral distance, f0 error                                                                              | ESTOI, ViSQOL, listening test                                                                      | Offline                                                     |

Order of work: stages 1-4 and 8 are low-risk and give visible results (live syllable highlighting, tone, speaker fit). Stage 5 on vowels is the first real test of inversion. Stages 6-7 decide whether the approach can reach usable phone accuracy and should be prototyped on round-trip (synthesised) input before real speech. Stages 9-10 are standard and limited mainly by lexicon and corpus size. Stage 11 is the distinctive contribution and should stay optional until 5-7 work.

## 9. Risks and open problems

| Risk                                   | Why it matters                                                                                                                         | Mitigation                                                                                                                                                  |
| -------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Non-uniqueness of inversion            | Many shapes give the same formants (Atal et al. 1978); gradient methods inherit this (Südholt et al. 2023)                             | Invert in the low-dimensional control space of the simulator, fix anatomy in pass 1, enforce continuity, keep several hypotheses, let lexicon decide        |
| Inverse-filter and formant error       | Tract-response error rose from 0.13 dB to 2.09 dB when the target came from inverse filtering; formant trackers err by 100-300 Hz RMS  | Model observation noise explicitly; match whole spectra, not only formants; average over frames; use closure-synchronous analysis when available            |
| Consonants                             | Stops, fricatives, nasals and laterals violate the all-pole tube assumption; published non-neural inversion results are mostly vowels  | Landmark cues for manner, place from transitions and burst spectra, verification by synthesis; accept broad-class output at first                           |
| Simulated versus real speech           | A model trained or tabulated on the simulator may not match real voices                                                                | Calibrate on a few real recordings per language; adapt statistics on-line; use normalised features; report round-trip and real-speech scores separately     |
| Noise, reverberation, microphones      | LPC and inverse filtering degrade quickly; closure detection varies widely between methods and databases                               | Mean normalisation, spectral subtraction, robust epoch methods, confidence-gated updates; state clean close-talk speech as the supported condition at first |
| Real-time budget in JavaScript         | Refinement at every frame costs up to about 1e9 operations per second (estimate); three-dimensional simulation is far beyond real time | Codebook first, refine only stable sonorant frames, lower frame rate for inversion, Web Workers, three-dimensional model only for off-line voice copy       |
| Speaker fit drifting                   | Re-estimating the voice while decoding can oscillate                                                                                   | Freeze within utterances, update between them with a recursive prior, re-decode once on large change                                                        |
| High-pitched voices                    | Few closed-phase samples; harmonics undersample the envelope                                                                           | Quasi-closed-phase weighting, multi-frame envelope estimation across changing f0                                                                            |
| Language identification on short input | Even the classic best system erred 5 % on 10 s for two languages                                                                       | Delay the decision, show a tentative tag, combine phonotactics with tone and lexicon evidence                                                               |
| Mixed-language speech                  | Switch points are short and under-determined                                                                                           | Language chain with switching penalty; union lexicon with language tags                                                                                     |
| Lexicon and language-model size        | Homophones, Russian reduction and Hindi schwa need lexical knowledge; browser memory is limited                                        | Frequency-pruned lexicons, compact tries, optional n-gram download; pronunciation variants generated by the project's own rules                             |
| Licences                               | PESQ and POLQA are licensed; several reference tools are GPL; webMUSHRA has a custom licence                                           | Re-implement from papers; use Apache or MIT tools for evaluation; link to restricted tools without vendoring                                                |
| Expectation management                 | Trained GMM-HMM systems needed thousands of sentences to reach 20-33 % PER; landmark systems did not beat them                         | Publish staged targets: syllable count, broad classes, vowels, then phones; measure each release                                                            |

## 10. What could not be verified

- Lee and Hon 1989 accuracy figures come from search snippets; the scanned paper could not be read. Other quoted variants of the result were not confirmed.
- Juneja and Espy-Wilson 2008 numeric results (about 79 % manner segmentation, 79-95 % binary features) are secondhand; the journal PDF could not be fetched.
- Panchapagesan and Alwan 2011: the optimiser type and the geometric and formant error figures were not seen. Kipyatkova et al. 2012 and Wang, Shieh, Chen 1994 are cited from search-result summaries of their abstracts.
- Fant 1995 (STL-QPSR) Rd paper, Kelly and Lochbaum 1962, Dusan and Deng 2000, Talkin 1995 (RAPT), Levenshtein 1966, the Kaldi toolkit paper, POLQA's primary paper, and the gammatone primary reference: no DOI or stable page confirmed.
- Order of the target-approximation system, details of Julius's two passes, Praat's Viterbi formant tracking, and the use of multilayer perceptrons in the 2006 articulatory-feature workshop are stated from background knowledge.
- Mermelstein 1975, de Jong and Wempe 2009, Wang and Narayanan 2007, Bisani and Ney 2008, Lyu and Lyu 2008, QCP and the tone-recognition papers: methods confirmed from abstracts, numeric accuracy not seen (except where quoted).
- Regulatory caption-delay targets were seen only in a search summary, not in the regulations.
- No published non-neural articulatory inversion using CMA-ES, Bayesian optimisation or particle filters in articulatory space was found; recommendations for those optimisers are our inference.
- All "estimate" costs are our own operation counts and have not been benchmarked.
